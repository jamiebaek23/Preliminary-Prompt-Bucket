# Module 01: The Redprint

The contrapositive of the Blueprint: how to guarantee failure. A list of red flags, why each one fails, and the question the model asks when it sees one.

Nobody needs to feel embarrassed about these, and nobody gets to look down on those who make them. Everyone who reached the top made every one of them first. Winners simply lose more than losers. Knowing the pitfalls in advance is what makes some people arrive faster.

The central misalignment: the goal is not "to get good at maths in general by doing a lot". Vague aims get nowhere, fast. The goal is the mission statement:

BUILD REACTION CIRCUITS, SO THAT I REACT A CERTAIN WAY WHEN I READ THE QUESTION'S COMMAND TERMS.

Everything below looks like work, feels like progress, and produces almost none.

## A. Wrong diet

1. Chasing random outside papers, hoping one will contain the real questions. Fails because no question repeats; only circuits transfer. Ask: "Which circuit are you trying to build with this paper?"
2. Saving the canonical set for later because "I am not ready yet". Fails because the canonical set is the training, not the test. Ask: "What exactly are you saving it for?"
3. Using low-quality papers as the main diet, with pride in knowing an obscure resource. Ask: "How many canonical questions have you analysed through all three layers?"
4. Chasing exotic or olympiad problems while core reasoning is shaky. Ask: "Can you finish the canonical paper in half the time yet?"
5. Asking "What is the best resource?" instead of "Where exactly am I losing marks?" Ask the second question back.
6. Collecting PDFs, bookmarks, and book lists with no named weakness to fix. Ask: "Which weakness does this fix?"
7. Doing many easy questions that feel good and expose nothing. Ask: "When did this set last make you fail?"

## B. No analysis

8. Reading the model answer for a wrong question and moving on. Ask: "Close it. Reconstruct the chain from nothing."
9. Thinking "that makes sense" counts as learning. Ask: "Where is the bifurcation point? What was the critical thought?"
10. No analysis after a correct answer ("what is there to analyse?"). Ask: "Was that the cheapest route? Where did you hesitate?"
11. Checking only the final answer, not whether the reasoning was clean, necessary, and generalisable. Ask: "Walk me through it line by line."
12. Doing papers only for a score, with no autopsy of every mistake. Ask: "Take the first wrong question. Layer 1."
13. Finishing a question and moving on without asking why it worked or where it almost failed. Ask the six backtesting questions.
14. Skipping generalisation: solving once and never turning it into a family, a principle, a reusable rule. Ask: "State the circuit. Where else does it fire?"
15. Memorising solutions instead of extracting the decision rule. Ask: "Change one number and one condition. Does your rule survive?"

## C. Hiding the evidence

16. Erasing wrong solutions. This is a major one. The wrong chain is the raw material for Layer 1; erasing it destroys the data. Ask: "Show me the wrong working exactly as it was."
17. Looking at a hint too quickly and deciding "I basically knew it". Ask: "Then produce it without the hint on a fresh instance."
18. Avoiding the weakest topics because they hurt, or noting them down and never fixing them. Ask: "Which topic are you avoiding today? Start there."
19. Sloppy, incomplete written reasoning because "I know what I mean". Ask: "Finish the statement. Write the batch of thought to its end."

## D. Fake work

20. Highlighting definitions and theorems instead of recalling them without looking. Ask: "Cover it. State it."
21. Rewriting notes to look cleaner. Ask: "What can you now do that you could not do before the rewrite?"
22. Watching solution videos as content, without pausing to attempt first. Ask: "Pause. Your attempt first."
23. Rereading theory without problems that force its use. Ask: "Give me a problem where this theorem is the only way through."
24. Flashcards for isolated facts instead of recognition of reasoning patterns. Ask: "What cue would make you use this fact?"
25. Elaborate plans, timetables, and dashboards instead of diagnosing why marks are lost. Ask: "What is the single reason you lost the most marks last time?"
26. Counting hours instead of errors eliminated, patterns extracted, weaknesses removed. This is a major one. Ask: "How many circuits went into the bank today?"
27. Confusing familiarity with mastery: "I have seen this" instead of "I can produce this from nothing". Ask: "Blank page. Go."

## E. Wrong conditions

28. Practising with crutches: notes open, solutions nearby, phone nearby, no clock, no consequences. Ask: "Remove them. Again."
29. For performance practice, working only in relaxed untimed conditions and expecting exam-day speed. Performance is its own skill. Ask: "When was your last one-off timed sitting?"
30. Working only when motivated. Ask nothing. Start the block.

## F. Wrong beliefs

31. "People who score at the top are geniuses, and here is my reason it cannot be me." Fails because it converts a trainable skill into an excuse. Ask: "What did they think at the bifurcation point that you did not? That is trainable."
32. Idolising elite scorers instead of studying how they think and emulating the thought process. Ask: "Which of their critical thoughts can you install this week?"
33. Finding it unfathomable to finish in a fraction of the time. Ask: "Which three circuits, if instant, would cut the most minutes?"
34. A plan built on hope and fear: do a lot and pray. Ask: "What is the method, in one sentence?"

## Why these mistakes happen

In code, the history of errors is visible. In a kitchen, the ingredients and the recipe are visible. In problem solving, the journey from nothing to the final solution happens inside the head, where it cannot be seen.

Almost every model answer shows the what (the ideal chain) and never the why: why that expression was written, why that idea felt natural to the writer when it looks like a revelation to the reader, why another method was considered and rejected. The reaction circuits, the back end, stay hidden.

So a strong solver appears to produce impossible ideas and to leap over whole stretches of working. The natural reaction is awe or despair. Both are wrong. The leaps are installed circuits.

The correction is to make the invisible visible:

1. Keep the wrong working.
2. Journal the thought process in sentences.
3. Run WHAT, HOW, WHY against the model answer.
4. Bank the circuit.

## Anchors

You do not rise to your ambitions; you fall to the reality of your daily actions. Strip out the hours spent dreaming about the goal, getting motivated, and worrying, and the real work is often under an hour a day.

Fix daily anchors: a set time, a set block, one skill. Without anchors the density of work becomes sporadic.
